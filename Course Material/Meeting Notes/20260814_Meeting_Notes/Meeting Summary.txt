Meeting Notes — Project Meeting #2
Project: Forecasting of Electricity Prices for Economic Model Predictive Control of Pumping in Water Supply Systems

1. Summary
The supervisor reviewed our progress and made one central point: filtering past data is not enough — we must build a genuine predictor with a prediction phase. Before the next meeting, all design reasoning and equations for the predictor must be written down. The predictor's real job is to extract the price trend and its maxima/minima so the EMPC knows when and how much to pump.
2. Key Points Discussed
2.1 A Prediction Phase Is Mandatory
Moving average / moving median filters only smooth and track past data. They have no predictive power. Conclusion: we have to build up a real predictor that forecasts future electricity prices, not just a filter.
2.2 Two Architecture Decisions to Be Made (and Written Down)
Decision A — Prediction scheme: recursive vs direct multi-step
Recursive prediction: predict one step, then feed the prediction back as pseudo-observation for the next step. Problems: errors accumulate; the covariance/matrix structure grows more complex with each step; computational cost increases with horizon. Example: $\hat{e}_{(t_0+2)|t_0}$ and $\hat{e}_{(t_1+1)|t_1}$ are both based on history, but with different recursion depths.
Direct multi-step prediction: build a model that directly maps current data to a forecast k hours ahead. Problem: is the model accurate enough that the prediction error stays small after several hours? If not, we have to recompute the prediction after some time steps.
Validation approach: check the prediction error against real data and find after how many steps our prediction starts to deviate significantly from reality. This number guides the choice between the two schemes.
Decision B — Recalculation frequency
Recalculate the prediction at every single time step, or every 30 minutes, or less frequently?
This is a trade-off between computational cost and freshness of the forecast, and it should follow from the error growth analysis of Decision A.
2.3 Prediction Horizon
We cannot predict only one step ahead. For pump optimisation we need forecasts for 6, 12, or 24 hours — or even more. The horizon choice must be justified.
2.4 Theoretical Prediction Error
A mathematical solution for the theoretical prediction error is required (e.g., error covariance propagation in recursive prediction; error bounds under model mismatch for direct prediction). This theory should later be checked against simulation results and real data.
2.5 Technical Approach
Try Prophet and Kalman filter for the next round of testing, and compare them.
HMM may be used for state judgement — not for prediction itself, but to decide which regime we are in (e.g., high-price vs low-price period, weekday vs weekend pattern). The state information can support the predictor.
The predictor's real job: solve out the trend and the maxima/minima of the trend. We do not need perfect point-by-point accuracy — we need to know when the price will be high and when it will be low, so we can solve when and how many pumps to use. This understanding should shape the predictor design and the error metrics.
Immediate experiment: use past data (e.g., from 1 Jan — whether more data is needed has to be tested) and predict the next 6/12/24 hours using the different methods.
Future benchmark: compare our predictor against AEMO's own forecast model and see which one gives better accuracy. Beating AEMO's forecast when tested against historical data is the success criterion of the project.
2.6 Documentation Habit
Record everything — all our thinking, equations, and simulation data. This material will form the basis of the future project report.
